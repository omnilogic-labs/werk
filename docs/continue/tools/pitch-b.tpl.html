The problem

Parallelism arrived. The cockpit did not.

Tabs are not a fleet view.

You started three agents this morning and two more after lunch. They are spread across your laptop, a box you ssh into and a container you provisioned on Tuesday. The only index of them is your memory, and your memory is wrong.

The expensive state is silence.

An agent that finished is fine. An agent that failed is fine, you will find it. The one that costs you an hour is the one that asked a polite question at 10:14 and has been sitting still ever since, while you assumed it was working.

Nobody knows what the team is running.

Multiply one engineer's five sessions by a team of twelve. There is no list. There is no way to answer "what is everyone's agent doing right now" other than asking everyone, one at a time, in Slack.

How it works

Four moves, and you are running a fleet.

01

Say where work happens.

Your Mac mini on the desk, a VPS, a container werk provisions, your company's Kubernetes cluster. Placement is a parameter, not an architecture. The same command and the same board, wherever the work lands.

02

Dispatch from your own terminal.

werk create builds a workspace: a place to run, a fresh git branch, your repo seeded there, and a terminal that keeps running when you shut the laptop. One self-contained binary, nothing to install on the remote.

03

Watch the board.

werk with no arguments prints every workspace on every machine, live. It is built to answer two questions and no others: which of these needs me, and what is that one doing right now.

04

Get the work back.

werk made the branch, put the code there and brings the commits home. A container destroyed with unpushed commits is a catastrophic bug in werk, not an edge case in your workflow. Getting the work back is the product.

Signals

werk can tell "thinking" from "asking you a question".

It does it without a single per-agent integration, because it does not watch the agent. It watches the terminal.

Well-behaved command-line programs already announce themselves. The bell tells werk something wants attention. OSC 9 and OSC 777 carry desktop notifications. OSC 9;4 reports progress. OSC 133 marks where one command ends, the next begins, and what the exit status was. Read those together and the difference between running, finished, failed and waiting on a human stops being a guess.

The consequence is the part worth caring about: an agent released next month, by a company that has never heard of us, shows up correctly on your board on the day you install it. No plugin, no adapter, no werk release. If it behaves like a terminal program, werk already understands it.

State Glyph Colour What werk saw
running Filled dot #2DD4A0 Output is moving.
needs you Filled dot with ring #FFB224 A bell, or a notification, and then nothing.
idle Hollow dot #5C6B7E Alive, silent for a while. Possibly forgotten.
failed Filled square #FF4D57 A command boundary with a non-zero exit status.

A closed vocabulary of four. Every state is carried by glyph and word as well as colour, so the board survives greyscale, a projector and colour blindness. Idle is deliberately the quietest thing on the screen.

The team portal

The wall.

Twelve engineers running five agents each is sixty terminals nobody can see. The team portal makes that one screen: every session in the company, grouped by team or repo or machine, colour-coded by state, live. A CTO opens it and reads the shape of the whole floor in three seconds. Then they drill into a single session and watch the actual terminal, scrollback and all, as it runs. Not a dashboard summarising work. The work itself, legible from across the room. This rung is roadmap, and it is the reason the rest of the ladder is built the way it is.

What makes one node the one you look at

It rolls up.

A parent carries the worst state underneath it, however deep. Dan's three sessions are all running. The amber on his row comes from a subagent two levels down that asked a question six minutes ago and has been sitting still since.

It floats.

Anything blocked on a human sorts above its siblings, and its ancestors sort above theirs. The rows that want something arrive at the top of the tree on their own. Nobody has to remember to filter.

Everything else goes quiet.

Idle greys out, done shrinks to a line, running is a dot and a word. The contrast is spent on the three rows that want a human, which is why those three are legible from the back of the room.

Placement

Your machines. Your sessions. Your infrastructure.

Run everything on hardware you already own and nothing leaves it. werk serve runs on your own machine: no account, no cloud and no third party in the path between you and a terminal on the box beside you.

One binary drives all four placements, so moving a workspace from your laptop to a cluster changes one argument and nothing else about the command or the board. The board does not care where the work is, which is the whole reason every machine can be on one.

Start

You already have a fleet. Get the board.

One binary, no runtime, nothing to install on the far end. Point it at your laptop, a box you ssh into, a container or a cluster, and the command and the board are identical in all four places. Start one workspace this afternoon, run werk with no arguments tomorrow morning, and see how many things were quietly waiting for you.

Questions

Questions, answered.

How many agents before this pays?

Two, on two different machines, is already more than you can hold in your head reliably. The board is the only place that knows where all of them are, what each one is doing and which of them stopped to ask you something. It scales in the direction you are already going: more agents, more machines, the same one command and the same board.

Where does the work actually run?

Wherever you point it: the Mac mini on the desk, a VPS you ssh into, a container werk provisions, your company's Kubernetes cluster. Placement is a parameter on the command rather than a different product, and the board reads the same for all four. Rungs one and two run entirely on your own machine, with no account and no third party in the path.

Does werk need to support my agent?

No. werk reads the signals any well-behaved terminal program already emits: bell, OSC 9 and 777 notifications, OSC 9;4 progress, OSC 133 command boundaries and exit status. Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI and Aider are all just command-line programs. An agent that ships next month works on the day you install it, with no release from us.

What happens to my commits if a container is destroyed?

werk creates the branch, seeds the repo and brings the commits back, and we treat losing them as a catastrophic bug rather than an edge case. Getting work back is not a feature bolted onto the side of the dashboard. It is half of what the product is for, and the board exists partly so that a workspace holding unpushed commits is something you can see rather than something you remember.

Brand appendix · Concept B

Flight Deck

werk is the flight deck for your agent fleet: every terminal, on every machine, on one live board, so you always know which one needs you.

The strategic bet

This concept wagers that the pain has already moved. Running one coding agent is solved by forty tools. Running twelve, across a laptop, a VPS, a container and a cluster, is not, and cross-machine terminal fleet dashboards are essentially unbuilt. We bet on the reader who has passed the threshold: the tech lead with eleven terminal tabs who cannot say which agent is stuck.

They win first because they feel the cost daily. It spreads upward, not sideways: the lead who runs the board wants their team on it, and the team portal is the thing a CTO buys. Fleet awareness is a wedge with a paid ceiling above it.

The mark

The Lattice. A 4 by 4 grid on a 24-unit artboard. Cells are 3-unit squares with a 1-unit corner radius, pitched 5 units apart, origins at 2, 7, 12 and 17. Fifteen are 1-unit strokes in Rule. One, at row 2 column 3, is a solid fill in Needs you, scaled to 1.35 about its own centre so it breaks the rhythm.

It reads at 16 pixels because at that size the grey lattice collapses to texture and only the amber cell survives, which is exactly the message: a fleet, and the one in it that wants you. Monochrome fallback is the fifteen cells at 40 per cent opacity and the one cell at full.

The wordmark is werk in Space Grotesk 700 at -0.025em, always lowercase, followed by a square status dot of 0.28em. The dot is the whole idea: on any live surface it carries the colour of the most urgent session in your fleet. On a static page it is Running green, and it never animates unless it is showing real state.

Typography

Family Weights Role
Space Grotesk 500, 700 Wordmark, headings, large numerals. Headings tracked at -0.02em.
Inter 400, 500, 600 Body, buttons, FAQ, navigation. Column labels at 11px, uppercase, 0.08em.
JetBrains Mono 400, 500, 700 The board itself: workspace names, placements, elapsed times, status words, commands.

The rule that makes the brand: anything the system knows is set in mono, anything we are telling you is set in Inter or Space Grotesk. A reader can tell fact from marketing by the shape of the letters. Elapsed times use tabular numerals so the column does not jitter as it ticks.

Taglines, ranked

  1. Know which one needs you.States the product's actual job in five words, presumes a fleet without arguing for one, and "needs you" is the pivot from anxiety to command. Quiet, and it never says "machines".
  2. One board. Every machine. Live.Says the differentiator out loud, which matters when the crowded local-only tools all sound alike. Describes the artefact rather than the relief, so it works better as a subhead.
  3. You are not running an agent. You are running a fleet.Reframes the reader's situation and creates the category. It is a sentence, not a mark, and it slightly scolds.
  4. The room you watch them from.Carries the whole feeling in six words and is the most memorable of the five. Alone it does not tell a cold reader what the software does.
  5. Twelve agents. Four machines. One screen.Concrete and instantly legible. The numbers date fast, and a reader running three agents reads it as "not for me", which is the wrong door to close.
